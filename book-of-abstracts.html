New Csound features, opcodes, and generative techniques

Shaping Musical Texture through Markov-Matrix Operations in Csound

Micael Silva

This paper presents four Csound opcodes for treating a Markov transition matrix as an explicit compositional object: BuildBiasedMatrix generates a matrix from intuitive affinity scores rather than typed probabilities, MatRotate cyclically shifts which state a hierarchy privileges, MatInterp interpolates between two matrices, and MatEntropy relaxes a matrix toward the maximum-entropy uniform matrix. These operations formalize procedures developed by hand across earlier textural compositions, now reusable across symbolic and audio-based work alike, regardless of the specific chain or material involved. Each operation is demonstrated through a short musical example built on a shared nine-state chain, with transition-matrix heatmaps, state timelines, and a Shannon-entropy curve offered as artifacts that consolidate the compositional intent behind each transformation. The result is a small, composition-first toolkit for shaping musical texture, situated within a practice-based approach to computer-assisted musical composition.

ARA and Cabbage, a patch made in heaven?

Rory Walsh

The Audio Random Access (ARA) extension has significantly expanded the capabilities of modern music production by allowing plugins access to entire audio regions. While highly powerful, developing ARA-capable plugins typically requires navigating dense, low-level APIs, a substantial barrier for audio researchers and open-source creators. This paper introduces a novel new ARA implementation within Cabbage 3. It focuses on the design patterns utilised within the core processing backend to negotiate host-to-plugin asset delivery, thread-safe memory sharing, and time-stamped playback markers within plugin environments. Furthermore, it explores how Cabbage 3 abstracts these low-level ARA callbacks, exposing the raw audio data and region metadata through dedicated Csound opcodes. This gives developers full access to the session timeline and audio assets using standard Csound syntax, enabling custom analysis and processing workflows without requiring complex ARA SDK knowledge.

Embedding Live Machine Learning in Csound: Emotion-Driven Harmony via an ONNX-Runtime Opcode

Jinlan Zhao, Li (Charlie) Shi, Richard Boulanger

Harmonic generation in Csound typically requires explicit chord specification in the score or coordination with an external process. We present the Affective Harmony Machine (AHM), a system that embeds an ONNX-format machine-learning model as a native Csound opcode, demonstrated through affect-driven chord progression generation. The system is backed by an annotated dataset of 108 jazz and pop chord progressions labeled with six emotion classes and seven scale/mode categories. A Random Forest classifier trained on this dataset is exported to the ONNX interchange format. The authors created emoChord, a Csound opcode that accepts an emotion string, performs live ML inference at i-rate via the ONNX Runtime C API, and schedules harmonically appropriate chord events into a synthesis instrument. No Python interpreter or external process is required at runtime; the current release targets macOS (arm64 natively, x86_64 via Rosetta). The implementation demonstrates a general pattern for embedding ONNX-compatible classifiers in Csound as a native plugin with no Python runtime requirement.

CStore: A Browser-Based Studio for AI-Generated and LLM-Revised Csound Instrument Design

Li (Charlie) Shi, Richard Boulanger

Most text-to-music and text-to-audio systems generate a finished waveform: something to listen to, but not something a composer can directly edit, version, or reproduce. CStore instead generates the underlying Csound project file, and unlike established Csound authoring environments such as CsoundQt and Cabbage, it is built around a generative agent trained on a large, curated corpus of Csound instruments. This paper presents the CStore Web Console, a browser-based studio built on top of that generator that lets a composer draft, audition, vary, and revise Csound instruments without leaving the keyboard. The console pairs a Next.js interface with a Flask sidecar that shells out to the Csound command-line renderer, and organizes work around a library of runs, single-key commands for drafting and playback, and two ways of producing variations: deterministic, score-derived “children” and LLM-driven variants tiered by how far they may drift from a “mother” run. A separate path lets a user hand a rendered instrument to one of six language-model providers with a plain-English instruction, such as “add a plate reverb and lower the pitch by an octave,” and get back a new, separately versioned .csd rather than an overwritten one. We describe the console’s architecture and workflow, and what building a studio around an editable .csd implies for how AI-assisted Csound instruments get made and revised.

NERD: A Node Editor for Csound Integration in Scientific and Artistic Contexts

Lovre Bogdanic

NERD (Node Editor for Real-Time DSP) is an open-source, Python-based visual programming environment that organises real-time digital signal processing pipelines as networks of heterogeneous, data-linked nodes on an interactive canvas. Its central feature is a first-class integration with Csound: synthesis instruments authored as standard .csd files are incorporated into any pipeline by adding inline comment annotations that declare their input and output channels; NERD’s meta-parser automatically exposes these channels as typed node ports without requiring any modification to the Csound instrument logic. Surrounding infrastructure—biosignal acquisition, motion tracking, MIDI, OSC, and LabStreamingLayer network streaming—is handled by a library of plugin nodes backed by a daemon-thread worker model. NERD originated in a (details omitted for blind review) research project on musical improvisation and joint action. It has since been used in several scientific and artistic events, including the Science Festival Frankfurt 2024 and the neuroscience outreach event Frankfurt hat Hirn 2026, and a live heart rate sonification installation during an artist-in-residence programme in 2025. The software is released under the GNU General Public License (v3) and is available at (details omitted for blind review).

Introducing Dr.C: 40+ Years of Csound and Computer Music, Now an Agentic AI Collaborator for Learning and Building Instruments, Effects, Plugins, and Web Apps

Mateo Larrea, Richard Boulanger

Dr.C is a domain-specialized AI system for Csound. It exists as two independent products built around the same philosophy. Dr.C CLI is a Bun-based terminal application, a two-column TUI with chat and version history on one side and a live CSD, waveform, and signal-flow view on the other, built for production: scripting, batch rendering, and shipping advanced audio-reactive web applications that open in a browser. Dr.C Standalone is an Electron desktop application built to teach: it ships with a free-tier model out of the box, keeps sound design and code preview inside one embedded window, and adds three things the CLI does not have. A thumbs up/down feedback loop and a store of durable, user-stated “lessons” quietly steer future generations toward a student’s own taste, without ever modeling how skilled that student is. A force-directed Knowledge Graph page turns 34 people, 15 institutions, 25 techniques, and a dozen landmark works of computer music history into something a student can pan, zoom, and get lost in. A Player page turns any finished .csd into a MIDI instrument on the spot, with MIDI Learn binding a hardware knob to a Csound channel the moment it moves. Both tools share the same curated retrieval over the full text of The Csound Book, the FLOSS Manual, the Cabbage manual and instrument collection, the McCurdy Csound and Cabbage instrument library, the Boulanger Csound Catalog, the Csound Journal and Csound Magazine, the canonical Csound reference manual, and four decades of instruments drawn from the Csound mailing list, the CsoundQt example library, and historic catalogues by Mathews, Risset, and Dodge and Jerse, ported to Csound; both propose alternatives rather than finished code; both narrate the history behind a technique as they teach it; and both export working Cabbage plugins. This paper describes what each tool is for, why we built two instead of one, and what early classroom use is teaching us about sequencing AI-assisted synthesis instruction when the interface itself is a pedagogical choice.

Csound Brain Lab: A Web Environment for Performing and Researching Brain-Computer Music Interfaces

Pedro Sodre, Richard Boulanger, Shiying (Amy) Chen, Wentao (James) Yuan, Xudong (Michael) Zhou, Luoxuan (Angela) Yu, Jiahe (Fancy) Fu and Yuwen (Liora) Meng

The Csound Brain Lab (CBL) is a web-based application that lets anyone get started performing and researching Brain-Computer Music Interfaces (BCMI). It combines the flexible audio-synthesis building blocks of browser-based Csound with convenient web-Bluetooth connectivity to Muse and OpenBCI headsets, behind a beginner-friendly interface that lets users visualize and listen to their own live brain data, or experiment with a bank of EEG simulators when no sensor is at hand. The system also embeds both remote and on-device large-language models that perform multimodal analysis of EEG and musical material, helping novices find meaningful correlations between brain activity and sound. We give an overview of the three tools the environment is built around, namely the EEG Source Panel, the Perform tab, and the Research tab; we discuss the strategies by which CBL turns a continuous brain-wave stream into musical phrases; and we reflect on how a system like this can put the human, whether the listener, the student, or the family member, back at the center of the computer-music experience.

Csound in the MetaVerse - Make It Yours: Personalizing Instruments, Worlds, and Live Sound in CsoundMeta

Strong Bear, Richard Boulanger

CsoundMeta is an immersive multiplayer environment, built in Unity for Meta Quest headsets, that lets performers touch, shape, and share Csound instruments as physical objects in a shared virtual or physical space. Since the system’s Orbs, Wanderers, and AI-generated Skybox worlds were first introduced, our central goal has shifted from handing performers a curated palette to play with, toward giving every Csounder the tools to build that palette themselves. This paper documents three developments that carry that goal forward. First, performers can now bring their own Csound work directly into the headset: any .csd file becomes an importable sound for an Orb or Wanderer, and Cabbage-authored .snaps files import as ready-to-use presets, so a patch built at a desk can be played, thrown, and squeezed the same evening. Second, the AI-generated Skybox worlds that once came from a fixed, curated set can now be prompted, remixed, and renamed from inside the headset itself, and every performer, whether standing shoulder to shoulder or joining from across the world, lands in the same generated room and can hand instruments to one another there. Third, and perhaps most consequential for ensemble work, we rebuilt the live audio pipeline around a native, low-latency engine that carries the microphone-to-effect-to-speaker signal path outside Unity’s own audio thread, bringing round-trip latency under 25 milliseconds, and introduced a dedicated, proximity-gated Mic instrument, passed hand to hand like any other object, and an overdub-capable sampler that can record from any source in the patch. We also describe the session tools, AI-generated backing tracks, and in-headset tutorials that keep a room full of personalized instruments and new performers from tipping into chaos. Together, these features move CsoundMeta from a system performers play inside toward a system they compose, build, and record in, one shaped as much by the Csound community as by its designers.

Utilizing Csound on embedded systems

Csound on Embedded Risc V and Xtensa Architectures

Aman Jagwani, Victor Lazzarini

With Csound 7, support for bare-metal microcontrollers with ARM Cortex CPUs was introduced. This allowed Csound applications to target these embedded platforms without OS dependencies for the first time, expanding the possibilities in application areas such as custom instrument design, interactive art installations, and DIY performance systems. This paper extends this work to two more embedded architectures, RISC-V and Xtensa, through the introduction of Csound for the ESP32 series of microcontrollers. These chips are widely available, very inexpensive, low-power, usually dual-core, and support extensive wireless networking capabilities. They also run FreeRTOS, providing an unexplored middle ground between bare-metal systems without schedulers and complete operating systems. This balance between extensive features and accessibility makes them an ideal target for expanding Csound’s range of supported platforms. In this paper, we describe the process of porting Csound to these platforms, highlight their benefits and limitations in relation to previously supported platforms, and discuss potential use cases and applications of Csound on these platforms.

Innovations in sound synthesis and processing techniques

OnLive 音: A Browser-Based Real-Time Audio Effects System Using Csound

Lihong Tao, Richard Boulanger

Many digital sound tools are powerful, but the technical barrier to entry in DAWs can make them hard to approach. This paper presents OnLive 音, a browser-controlled real-time audio effects console for live input and uploaded samples, built on Csound with a React performance interface. It treats real-time audio processing like a pedalboard rather than a production suite: the user picks an audio source, toggles effects, adjusts controls, and hears the result immediately, without having to build a full digital audio workstation (DAW) session. And every effect can be opened to reveal the Csound .csd file behind it, so users can see exactly how each sound is made and start learning Csound itself. This paper explores how a browser interface can make the depth of Csound directly playable in live performance, through the design and validation of OnLive 音.

An Analysis-Guided Workflow for Constructing Playable Csound Physical Models: A Tupipa Case Study

Yao Ma, Wanjun Yang

The Tupipa is a traditional three-string plucked lute from southwest China whose sound is preserved mainly through recordings and documentation. Its three strings form two courses: a double course tuned in unison and a single-string course, the latter providing a controlled first case for playable modeling. Such materials document performance practice but do not provide playable controls for physical-model synthesis. This paper presents an analysis-guided workflow for constructing playable Csound physical models from recordings, supplemented by limited physical measurements. The workflow combines ATS and time-domain analysis of recordings with rule-based parameter initialization, diagnostic acoustic-descriptor comparison, bounded local parameter search, and listening-panel calibration. The case study therefore focuses on the single-string course and links measured plucking position, ATS F0, partial balance, decay, and residual/noise cues to Csound controls. In the model-construction stage, initialized controls are instantiated as a four-layer Csound instrument: a wgpluck2 core string-vibration layer, a plectrum-contact layer, a friction/noise layer, and a body-response layer. Rather than introducing another instrument-specific synthesizer, the study shows how recordings can become playable, physically interpretable Csound instruments while retaining listening judgment in the final calibration stage.

Four Routes Through Hyperspace: Wavetable Navigation Strategies via Givens Rotations

Luca Bimbi, Anthony Di Furia, Giuseppe Silvi

This paper presents an approach to navigating the hyperspace of a wavetable, interpreted as a vector in ℝN, based on Givens rotations. A discrete audio signal of N samples is treated as a point in N-dimensional Euclidean space; Givens rotations, acting on pairs of coordinates through the sine and cosine of an angle, displace this point while preserving the L2 norm. Four strategies for selecting rotation pairs and angles are proposed and compared: a local adjacent chain, a non-local prime step, Sobol quasi-random sequences, and a Kuramoto coupled-oscillator model. Each strategy is realized both as an interactive Python prototype and as a Csound plugin opcode operating on 1024-sample wavetables.

A Vision-to-Music System for Accessible Interactive Sound Experiences Using Computer Vision and Csound

Will Chen, Richard Boulanger

This paper presents a real-time vision-to-music system designed to provide an accessible and interactive auditory experience for people with visual impairments. A webcam captures the surrounding environment while a Python application using OpenCV [7] extracts object position, relative size, and dominant color. These visual features are transmitted to Csound using Open Sound Control (OSC) [6], where five synthesis instruments generate a continuously evolving musical soundscape. Object position controls stereo panning and musical pitch, while relative object size modifies filter cutoff, reverberation, and delay. The proposed system demonstrates how computer vision and real-time sound synthesis using Csound [1] can provide an alternative form of environmental interaction beyond traditional speech-based assistive technologies.

Binaural Explorer: Theta. A Csound & Cabbage-based System for Real-Time Auditory Entrainment

Yuqi Wu, Richard Boulanger

This paper presents Binaural Explorer: Theta, a cross-platform Cabbage plugin that embeds precise theta-frequency binaural beats directly into existing music, generative soundscapes, and live DAW environments. Leveraging the Csound synthesis engine, the plugin applies a real-time spectral phase vocoder to superimpose a neurologically-active theta differential (4.0–7.2 Hz) onto arbitrary audio signals without degrading the underlying sonic structure. Users can substitute source tracks, activate a fully binauralized generative composition mode, and fine-tune target beat frequencies via granular controls. The system includes dynamic visual feedback (a pulsing “beating cabbage” graphic scaled to the live theta rate), an interactive educational mode, and a linked informational pane for psychoacoustic study. A small early-adopter cohort reported reduced sleep latency, enhanced study focus, and a striking expansion of the perceived soundstage. Functioning as both a research utility and a pedagogical toolkit, this readily-customizable open-source tool supports informal clinical research, interactive sonification, and meditation practice.

A Rapid Opcode Development Workflow for Real-Time Physical Modelling in Csound

Juliette Tudoce, Vasileios Chatziioannou, Alex Hofmann

Implementing a custom physical modelling opcode in Csound requires fluency in C programming, a skill that remains a significant barrier for many musicians and researchers in music acoustics, despite the strong potential of physics-based sound synthesis. This paper presents a rapid development workflow for creating a Csound opcode. We use the case study of the Đàn Bầu, a Vietnamese monochord instrument plucked by the right hand and pitch-bended by the left hand through a stem that modulates the tension of the string. Starting from a generic string model drawn from the acoustics literature, we first prototyped it in Python, allowing for straightforward implementation and assessment of the energy balance. We then translated the Python code into C with the help of Large Language Models (LLMs), reducing the time typically required for this transition. Finally, the resulting C code was manually adapted into an opcode plugin, enabling its integration into Csound. This case study demonstrates that the complete process from mathematical model to performable instrument through Csound can be achieved rapidly, even by someone without deep C programming expertise. The workflow presented here offers a replicable template for musicians and researchers in music acoustics wanting to develop other physical modelling opcodes for real-time implementation in Csound.

Performer skills in Live-electronics

Spatial Agents: A Timbre-Driven Ambisonic Instrument for Live Electronics in Csound and Max

Alberto Maria Gatti, Francesco Perissi

Spatial Agents is a live-electronics instrument built on a single premise: that the place of a live sound should follow its timbre. Five piezoelectric contact microphones on a hand-built resonating box are analysed in real time into five audio descriptors, and those descriptors move five autonomous sources, or agents, through an Ambisonic field decoded to a horizontal loudspeaker ring or to headphones. The paper is as much about a compositional stance as about a system. Following a tradition of spectral and timbre spatialisation, we treat space not as a container for sound but as a dimension the performer shapes directly: a still, pure resonance settles near the centre of the field, a bright gesture rises, a noisy evolving texture is thrown to the periphery and set spinning. We describe how the descriptors map to motion, focusing on a mapping in which spectral stillness and purity pull a source inward while spectral change pushes it outward, and we explain why we run Csound as the engine inside Max rather than Csound alone: the pairing joins Csound’s analysis and synthesis vocabulary and its portable, sample-accurate processing to the live routing, control and visual feedback that a performance patch needs. The Ambisonic decoder uses the ACN/SN3D convention and is rebuilt at run time from a description of the loudspeaker layout. The approach adapts a descriptor-driven spatialization model previously introduced for corpus-based improvisation; what is new here is a self-contained Csound engine, built from scratch and driven live by a physical contact-microphone instrument rather than a pre-analysed corpus. The instrument is functional and has been validated; we close with the compositional questions it opens and with work still in progress.

One Environment, One Chain: Csound's arduinoRead as a Native Sensor Interface for Performance-Oriented IMU Control

Jinlan Zhao, Richard Boulanger

Live performance leaves little room for a sensor pipeline that misfires. When a violinist’s gesture is intended to trigger one sound but produces two, the error is immediate and audible, and mapping design alone cannot fully compensate for it. This paper presents L-Bow, a wrist-worn six-degree-of-freedom (6-DoF) inertial measurement unit (IMU) controller for augmented violin performance, designed and built by the author, and asks whether the sensor-to-sound pipeline can be made more reliable by removing an intermediate software layer. L-Bow integrates gesture sensing, six performance modes, and a shared effects chain within a single self-contained Csound file, using Csound’s native arduinoRead opcode for Arduino communication rather than a Python serial parser and OSC bridge. A controlled trigger test comparing the two workflows showed a modest latency advantage for the native path. The paper discusses what this means for designing dependable, low-maintenance performance systems, and where the native approach still falls short.

Algorithmic composition and sound processing techniques with Csound

csonus: An open-source collection of opcodes for algorithmic composition and experimental sound design

Valerio Orlandini

While the Csound ecosystem provides a vast library of tools for synthesis and signal processing, practitioners in real-time algorithmic composition and experimental sound design often encounter a gap in native, instrument-level opcodes for complex generative processes. This limitation frequently forces composers into hybrid workflows with external environments, introducing latency and synchronization overhead. This paper introduces csonus, an open-source collection of C++ opcodes designed to bridge this gap by integrating algorithmic composition and sound generation techniques directly into the Csound instrument block. Built using the Csound Plugin Opcode Framework (CPOF), the package provides efficient, real-time implementations of diverse tools, including chaotic systems, cellular automata, Lindenmayer systems, and neural network-driven synthesis. By enabling tightly coupled, multi-level algorithmic decision-making and experimental sound manipulation without external dependencies, csonus fosters a more integrated workflow for both studio and live-electronics contexts. The library is written in modern C++ and is publicly available under the MIT license.

From Brainwaves to Csound: An Exploratory Audiovisual System with EEG and LLM-Based Agent Control in Unity

Chuhan Gao, Richard Boulanger, Mateo Larrea Ferro

We present an exploratory audiovisual biofeedback system that couples real-time EEG, a large language model (LLM) agent, and Csound, turning a listener’s brain activity into evolving sound and a synchronized visual scene. NeurOSC, a Python program, extracts five EEG band powers and broadcasts them over OSC; inside Unity, a platform for creating real-time, interactive 3D content, these become baseline-normalized features and one of five mental states. Two paths then drive a continuously running Csound instrument through CsoundUnity: a continuous path maps brain features to synthesis parameters every frame, while the LLM agent makes larger, coordinated changes on state transitions and shapes a matching visual scene. We report two contributions: parameter auto-discovery, by which a self-describing Csound instrument (a Cabbage header) can be controlled by the agent with no manual registration; and a two-path mapping pairing continuous biofeedback with discrete agent actions. Running with both OpenBCI and Muse headsets, the system is an early step toward treating physiological signals as an expressive, real-time control source for Csound.

Exploring Csound's educational potential

Orchestrating an Interpreter: Csound7 as a Host for Symbolic Programs

Hlöðver Sigurðsson

This paper evaluates whether Csound7’s Orchestra language is mature enough to host complex symbolic programs, not just sound and music systems. As a case study, I implemented a Clojure-inspired Lisp interpreter entirely in Orchestra using the Make-A-Lisp process. The interpreter exercises recursive structs, typed user-defined opcodes, arrays, dynamic compilation, lexical environments, closures, macros, tail calls, exceptions, interactive input, and Csound-native musical constructs. The work exposed defects in type propagation, nested struct arrays, overload selection, pass-by-reference semantics, recursion safety, and default initialization, leading to reduced tests and improvements in Csound7. The main remaining limitation is memory lifetime: temporary recursive values persist until the enclosing instrument ends, preventing reliable self-hosting. The result shows that Csound7 can support substantial symbolic programs close to its music engine, while allocation-heavy programs still require better lifetime control.

Unleashing creativity and fostering interdisciplinary collaborations through Csound projects

A Toolkit for Empirical Impulse Response Preparation

Victor Lazzarini

Empirical impulse responses can be obtained by measuring physical systems. Csound has built-in means for utilising these in musical applications through its various types of convolution opcodes. Until now, it lacked out-of-the-box means for preparing empirical impulse responses, although this can actually be implemented from first principles in Csound code. This paper introduces a native toolkit developed for this purpose, in three forms: a utility, a GEN, and opcodes.

Immersive Engagement: Building the "Wildtrace" Sonic World with AI Assistance

Haokun Yang, Wanjun YangPresenter: Miao Zhang

Addressing the bottlenecks of high cost and long cycles in custom audio plugin development, this study proposes an agile workflow based on the synergy between Csound and Large Language Models (LLMs). On this basis, we developed ”Wildtrace”, an algorithm-driven quadraphonic spatial audio plugin suite. This paper systematically elaborates its development logic and AI collaboration mechanism, providing a novel paradigm for agile audio prototype development in resource-constrained environments.

Neural Virtual Analog Modeling in Csound via Real-Time Recurrent Inference

Ken Kobayashi

Csound’s native virtual analog opcodes rely on fixed algorithmic structures that cannot learn transfer functions from empirical data or capture physical analog hardware. We present a real-time neural inference pipeline integrating Csound’s C++ Plugin Opcode Framework (CPOF) with the RTNeural engine, executing stateful recurrent networks directly within Csound orchestra syntax. We evaluate the pipeline on two targets: a parameterized 4-pole Moog ladder filter trained on oversampled numerical simulations, and a fixed-setting analog distortion pedal trained on physical loopback recordings. For the filter, shortening the loss-masked training warmup to 256 samples forces rapid convergence from a cold hidden state, enabling click-free note-on with only a brief output ramp; reducing the warmup further to 128 samples reduces static ESR at the 100 Hz cutoff by 7.6 dB. A multi-rate LFO training regime addresses dynamic tracking degradation: a static-trained 128-unit baseline degrades by 17.3 dB under a 5 Hz cutoff sweep, while multi-rate LFO training compresses rate-dependent variance to 3.6 dB across 1–20 Hz on held-out audio. The same recurrent architecture, without the cutoff input, achieves −29.3 dB held-out ESR and −37.9 dB aliasing-to-signal ratio at 8 kHz on the hardware distortion pedal when trained via pre-emphasized ESR on loopback recordings. Deployed 128-unit GRU opcodes consume under 17% CPU per voice, sustaining 6-voice polyphony at 48 kHz.

Real-time implementation of Csound

Real-Time Song Generation: A Csound API Case Study

Garrett Eckl, Steven Yi, Edward Costello, Leonardus Van Der Ploeg

This case study presents RIFFIT, a commercial text-to-song platform that utilizes the Csound audio programming language. This platform enables users to turn any text into a fully-produced song in near real-time, with a variety of genres and voice models to select from. Csound serves as the platform’s primary synthesis engine, performing a custom CSD file that is dynamically generated based on both the inputted text and selected musical preferences. This paper begins with a brief introduction to the field of automatic music generation, which is followed by an overview of the RIFFIT platform and key developments that have occured to date. An outline of the software’s architecture is then provided, with topics including the dynamic generation of Csound code, the manual performance of Csound files via the Csound API, and strategies for the web deployment of Csound-enabled software. Lastly, an analysis of Csound’s suitability to such commercial applications is discussed.

Contributions engaging with the work and legacy of Gottfried Michael Koenig and James Tenney

The Process of Constructing Postcard from Sertões within a Csound Environment

Catarina Rossi, Bruno Gazoni

This paper describes the creative and technical process of the performance Postcard from Sertões. The work details the transposition of Almeida Prado's Sertões (1978) for solo viola [1] into the interactive electroacoustic domain, inspired by the compositional economy of James Tenney's Postal Pieces (1971) [2]. The research was developed within an Interpretive Experimentation Laboratory, which consisted of distilling Prado's transtonal system into four essential sound blocks, interpreted as "clangs", primary perceptual units based on Tenney's theory. The performance establishes a bridge between written tradition and orality by distributing musical materials between the Brazilian rabeca and the viola. The implementation, done via Csound and Cabbage, involves a real-time capture and Digital Signal Processing (DSP) system, operated by a postcard-inspired visual interface. In this setup, a second musician manipulates the audio input through four looper tracks, one for each clang, enhanced by effects applied to the repetitions to reshape their identities. The laboratory validated the interaction protocol between the instrumentalist and the software operator through remote testing and in-person rehearsals. The analysis concludes that technological mediation promotes a transformation of the work, resulting in a new hybrid composition that functions as a "virtual postcard," reactivating the symbolic resonances of the sertão through a phenomenological and immersive listening zone.

Somatic Continuum Paper

Tyler Kaufman

Somatic Continuum is an interactive installation in which a visitor's face, hands, heartbeat, and presence become the instrument. There are no buttons and nothing to hold. A camera reads the face through MediaPipe FaceMesh, a hand tracker reads the hands, and a worn heart monitor reads the pulse. All of it drives a live Csound orchestra of more than twenty instruments over Open Sound Control. The instruments are spread across nine worlds, and each world puts a different instrument on each hand. A shared D minor field keeps every combination in tune. The visitor's heartbeat pulses the image and sets the timing of the arpeggios, so image and sound stay in time with the visitor's pulse. Each person who enters brings a new sonic world into being. Nothing is recorded in advance. The music is generated live by rules as the body shapes it. Koenig and Tenney composed by setting rules and letting them generate the music; Somatic Continuum does the same, except the rules run in real time and the body sets them in motion. The work gives Csound a new kind of interface, one played with the body and run live from a web layer, that lets an untrained visitor compose by moving.

Integration of Csound into web-based applications

pch2csd: From Command-Line Converter to Web-Based Modular Synthesis Environment

Gleb Rogozinski

The pch2csd project, initiated in 2015, preserves the sonic heritage of the Clavia Nord Modular G2 synthesizer by converting its patches into Csound code. This paper presents the evolution of pch2csd into a web-based modular synthesis environment that combines the proven Python conversion engine with a real-time visual patching interface. We detail the system architecture, the reuse of the existing codebase through a Flask API, and the design decisions enabling seamless interaction between the visual patch editor and the Csound audio engine. The result is a platform that not only preserves the Nord Modular heritage but extends it into browser-based sound design, allowing users to load, visually edit, and hear patches directly in a web browser without proprietary software.

Evoking Harmony via Convolution

Michael Gogins

I demonstrate how to impart, or evoke, the tonality of a chord from a source sound while, at the same time, minimizing artifacts and creating an interesting effect. Conceptually, the effect consists of convolving the source signal with the pitch-classes of the chord. Paranoise is a performance for analog synthesizer and Csound live processing. Transmutational Interplay for Piano and Csound is a solo piano piece reshaped live by a second performer processing the piano signal in real time on CsoundLive, a browser-based Csound 7 performance environment built around 22 time-based and 12 spectral effects, MIDI-mapped controls, and an internal live-input, sampler, and player mixing architecture. Electropoem No.12

Ali Balighi

Fixed Media

The Electropoem series is a collection of multichannel compositions that explore the connections between sound, space, and poetic expression. Each piece focuses on the spatial placement and movement of sound to create an engaging listening experience where the balance between sound and silence encourages attentive listening.

The multichannel format allows sounds and textures to move dynamically within a defined space. This approach helps create clear and precise auditory environments, making it easier for listeners to focus on the details of the music.

Electropoem No. 12 is based on sounds recorded by NASA on Mars. This includes 16 minutes of raw, unfiltered sounds captured by the Perseverance rover as it traveled through Jezero Crater. The recordings feature noise generated by the interaction between the rover’s wheels, suspension, and the Martian surface, as well as a high-pitched scratching noise. Notably, the microphone used for these recordings was originally designed for entry, descent, and landing operations and had limited testing for surface recordings before the rover's launch.

Mars’s atmosphere affects how sound behaves. Because it is cold and thin, sound travels at about 540 mph (240 m/s), which is slower than the 760 mph (340 m/s) on Earth. The atmosphere, mostly made up of carbon dioxide (96%), absorbs higher-pitched sounds, allowing only lower frequencies to carry over long distances. These factors result in soft, filtered sounds that shape the character of the piece.

The work also uses the ED2-07 tuning system, which divides an octave into seven equal parts. The scale is as follows:

171.42857, 342.85714, 514.28571, 685.71429, 857.14286, 1028.57143, 2/1

This tuning system, combined with the unique Martian soundscapes, adds variety to the series and introduces new musical possibilities. By combining these elements, Electropoem No. 12 highlights the potential for new approaches to music using unfamiliar sound sources and tuning systems.

P l e n u m (Groot Auditorium Leiden)

Michele Del Prete

Fixed Media · 10'13"

P l e n u m is a series of pieces devoted to the exploration of site- and instrument-specific constellations through electronics realized exclusively in Csound. The series investigates the phantasm of the organ’s full presence—a sonic effect traditionally achieved, in its ideal form, through the plenum: the organ registration in which several, potentially all, aliquot stops are added to the principal.

For P l e n u m (Groot Auditorium, Leiden) I recorded several sound combinations of the 1998 Flentrop organ at Groot Auditorium, an instrument built after 17th-century Dutch models. The compositional process involves creating a plurality of microtonally shifted textures in which the recorded material is transposed by gradually increasing factors and enriched through multiple artificial plenum gradations. The aliquot strata are algorithmically generated to replicate the mixture structure of the Flentrop organ. At the formal level, echo sections play a key role, as they do in several Fantasias by Jan Pieterszoon Sweelinck.

In the co-presence of multiple organs within the same space, the question of the polyphony of space becomes immediately striking. Yet even within the spatiality of a single organ, the multiplicity of sound sources remains a crucial issue. Polyphony is a fundamental category of organ music; organ placement is an extremely complex subject, and the question of sound projection in the electronic realm is itself immense. A kind of mise en abyme therefore emerges in the relationship between organs, electronics, and spaces.

I conceive my P l e n u m series as a contribution to this exploration.

Fixed Media

This composition investigates the notion of technological and computational obsolescence as a lens through which to reflect on human obsolescence, particularly in relation to mechanical, artistic, and intellectual labor. Within a social and economic climate increasingly oriented toward efficiency and automation, human beings are placed in constant competition with artificial systems designed to be faster, tireless, and free from the limitations that characterize human labor. Porphyry v7.4.2

Michael Gogins

Fixed Media

This fixed-media electroacoustic work is rendered in Csound using an orchestra that generates pipe-organ audio with Modartt’s Organteq plugin and processes it with the Mverb2020 reverberation plugin. Organteq is a physical model of a pipe organ and does not use samples. The entire score is generated and rendered by a single Python script. Bars of music from the musical dice game traditionally attributed to Mozart are played in sequence, but each bar is repeated a randomly chosen number of times, as in Terry Riley’s In C. Every few bars, the music progresses to a randomly selected chord. Periodically, the current chord is used as a pivot for modulation to a new diatonic scale, and the generated notes are quantized to that scale. The Organteq registration is also changed periodically.
